The Greek property market has changed more in the last ten years than in the twenty before. After the crisis years it entered a long phase of recovery, driven by tourism, foreign demand and new rules on tax and residency. Here are the trends that shape the market in 2026, what each one means for a buyer, and where the risks are.

## Short answer

- **A long upswing since 2017.** The current cycle began with an expansion phase in 2017; coastal and tourist areas have led it.
- **Coast and tourism set the prices.** Coastal areas are generally more expensive than inland areas because of beaches, resorts and rental demand.
- **Holiday letting** has become a major use of property, and is now regulated.
- **Policy matters more than ever:** Golden Visa zones, the VAT suspension on new builds, the capital gains suspension and the announced 15 % tax for non-EU buyers.
- **Greener and more digital homes**, and a buying process that can largely be done from abroad.

## 1. The real estate cycle

Property markets move in cycles: expansion (rising prices and sales), peak (stable prices), contraction (falling demand) and recession (lowest prices). Greece went through a deep contraction during the crisis years, when many buyers stayed away and prices fell sharply. The most recent cycle began in 2017 with an expansion phase: more sales, rising demand for homes and, in the most popular areas, rising prices.

In Halkidiki we have seen the same pattern: prices in coastal areas have risen steadily since 2017, as Thessaloniki airport added direct flights from Germany, the UK and the Balkans. Cycles do turn, which is why we show the sales history of an area rather than promising appreciation. Our article on [Halkidiki market trends](/en/articles/buyers/halkidiki-real-estate-market-trends-what-to-expect-in-the-coming-years) looks at the region in detail.

## 2. Coast versus inland

Prices vary widely between regions and even between neighbouring villages. Coastal areas tend to cost more than inland ones because of their proximity to beaches, resorts and tourist services. The state's own rules reflect the hierarchy: for the Golden Visa, Attica, Thessaloniki, Mykonos, Santorini and islands with more than 3,100 residents require an investment of €800,000, while areas such as Halkidiki are in the €400,000 zone.

Inland villages offer a different deal: lower prices, stone houses and life all year. In Halkidiki, a renovated stone house in Arnaia or Parthenonas usually costs less than a comparable house close to a Kassandra beach. See our guide to [villages worth knowing](/en/articles/all-news/hidden-gems-lesser-known-villages-in-halkidiki-worth-exploring).

## 3. Tourism and short-term rentals

Tourism is a pillar of the Greek economy, and it shapes the property market directly. Many buyers now combine personal use with letting: a few weeks for the family, the rest of the season for guests. This has raised demand for well-located homes near beaches and for properties that are easy to manage.

Letting has also become more regulated. Short-term rentals need a registration number (ΑΜΑ), guests pay a climate resilience fee per night, and rental income is taxed separately: 15 % up to €12,000, 25 % up to €24,000, 35 % up to €35,000 and 45 % above, after a 5 % expense deduction. Owners of up to two short-term properties pay no VAT. The full rules are in our [property tax guide](/en/articles/business-news/taxes-foreigners-properties-greece).

## 4. Foreign demand and the policy response

International buyers have been a big part of the recovery. According to figures presented by the government, buyers from outside the EU invested about €1.2 billion in Greek property in 2025, about €800 million of it in residential real estate.

The government's response in 2026 is the announced 15 % transfer tax on residential purchases by non-EU/EEA buyers from 1 July 2027, presented as part of its housing policy. It is not law yet; today everyone pays 3.09 %. Read what it would mean in our article on the [2027 transfer tax for non-EU buyers](/en/articles/buyers/greece-property-transfer-tax-non-eu-buyers-2027).

## 5. New builds and the VAT suspension

New buildings would normally carry 24 % VAT. The suspension, extended until 31 December 2026 by Law 5246/2025, means buyers of qualifying new builds pay the 3.09 % transfer tax instead. This has supported new developments, especially small complexes and villas in tourist areas. See [what the VAT suspension means for you](/en/articles/buyers/the-new-regulation-relief).

## 6. Sustainable architecture and green homes

Buyers increasingly look at energy class, insulation, solar panels and heat pumps. Every sale needs an energy certificate, so the information is in front of you, and certified green buildings get a 5–10 % discount on ENFIA. For a holiday home that is empty part of the year, low running costs and good insulation count as much as the view.

## 7. Tradition and modern design

Greek architecture is rich in tradition, and many buyers want it: stone houses, tiled roofs, courtyards. In protected traditional settlements, such as Arnaia in Halkidiki, renovation rules apply to facades, and your engineer checks what is allowed. At the other end, contemporary villas with large glass surfaces and infinity pools are the fastest-growing part of the luxury market. The best projects combine the two: modern interiors inside a traditional shell.

## 8. Changing buyers and housing needs

The buyer profile is broader than before. Besides holiday-home buyers we see people who work remotely and want to live by the sea for longer periods, and many who let the house for several summers and move in when they retire. This pushes demand towards villages that live all year, with schools, shops and doctors, and not only towards summer resorts.

## 9. Technology and the role of the agent

Video viewings, online listings with full photos and exact locations for registered members, digital land registry checks and online tax services have changed how property is bought. Agents now spend less time showing photos and more time checking paperwork, organising viewings and coordinating lawyers, notaries and engineers. Social media helps a property reach a wide audience quickly, but buyers still need someone who answers their questions honestly. More on this in our article on [technology in Greek real estate](/en/articles/all-news/digital-transformation-the-role-of-technology-in-greek-real-estate).

## Challenges and opportunities

The market faces real uncertainties: changes in tax rules, the dependence of holiday areas on tourism, and global events that affect buyers' incomes. Two suspensions (VAT on new builds and capital gains tax for private sellers) end on 31 December 2026 unless extended. The opportunity is that areas such as Halkidiki still offer homes by the sea at relatively affordable prices compared with other popular Mediterranean destinations and the big islands, with a long season and clear buying procedures.
